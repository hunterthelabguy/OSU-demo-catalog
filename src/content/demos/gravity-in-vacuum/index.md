## Physics

Gravity accelerates all objects at the same rate; what separates a feather
from a marble in everyday experience is air resistance. A falling object
collides with air molecules, and the resulting drag force points opposite to
the motion, growing with speed (roughly proportional to speed when slow and to
speed squared when fast). When drag balances gravity the object stops speeding
up: it has reached terminal velocity, which depends on mass and shape. The
marble is dense and aerodynamic, so its terminal velocity is far higher than
anything it reaches in a short drop. The feather is light with a large surface
area, so it hits its low terminal velocity almost immediately and drifts. Pump
the air out and both fall identically, exactly as they would on the Moon.

## Setup

Attach the vacuum pump to the tube's valve and evacuate the chamber. Confirm
the marble and feather both move freely inside before class.

## Procedure

1. With air still in the tube, tip it over: the marble drops, the feather
   drifts. Collect the prediction for the evacuated case before pumping.
2. Evacuate the tube, tip it again, and let the room see both land together.
3. Close by asking what changed: not gravity, only the air.

## Pedagogical notes

Run predict-first. The strongest discussion comes from students who predict
"closer, but the marble still wins," which surfaces the belief that mass
itself, not drag, sets the fall rate. The Apollo 15 hammer-and-feather drop on
the Moon is the standard transfer case.
